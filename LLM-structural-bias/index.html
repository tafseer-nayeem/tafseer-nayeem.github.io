01 / The question

When a model says “English,” which English does it mean?

Widely used AI products often present “English (US)” as a default. This study asks how that preference takes shape inside language models: does it begin in their data, appear in the way they represent language, and carry through to generated text?

We use British English as a controlled reference variety and examine American-English preference across the model development pipeline. The comparison makes a familiar regional contrast measurable; it is not a proxy for the full diversity of English worldwide.

RQ1

Data exposure

Where does an American-English advantage appear in training and alignment data?

RQ2

Representation

Does that advantage persist in tokenization and model prediction cost?

RQ3

Generation

Does American English emerge as the default in model outputs?

02 / The method

A paired comparison across the pipeline

The study builds a curated resource of 1,813 matched American-English and British-English variants. The pairs support comparable measurements across corpora, tokenizers, model likelihoods, and generated responses.

DIALIGN

DIALIGN is a dynamic, training-free method for estimating regional alignment. It aggregates local distributional evidence from overlapping text spans, including lexical, grammatical, structural, stylistic, and multi-word patterns.

On a balanced benchmark of 1,500 news passages, DIALIGN reaches 93.18% accuracy and 93.38 F1.

03 / The evidence

The preference appears at every stage

Across the analyses, American English is favored in data exposure, generally encoded more compactly, assigned lower prediction cost, and produced more often under neutral English prompting.

01

It begins in the data

All six audited pretraining corpora and all 21 post-training datasets favor American-English variants. Across the post-training data, 76.17% of 28.86 million matched-variant occurrences are American English.

6 / 6corpora favor AmE
02

It appears in representation

American-English forms are generally tokenized more compactly. In paired, semantically matched sentences with equal token counts, all ten evaluated checkpoints assign higher prediction cost to the British-English counterfactual.

10 / 10checkpoints show higher BrE cost
03

It carries into generation

Under neutral English prompting, a majority of outputs are classified as American English across the evaluated models and tasks. British-English prompting shifts outputs, but does not consistently remove the preference.

AmEdominant neutral-prompt default
04 / Why it matters

Defaults are built into systems

The results point to a pipeline-wide pattern rather than a generation-only quirk. Data composition, tokenizer design and lineage, post-training choices, and evaluation practices can all shape which variety is treated as ordinary.

This evidence motivates targeted audits and interventions at each stage, and raises questions about linguistic homogenization, representation, and equitable deployment. The study establishes consistent associations across the evaluated pipeline; it does not claim that any single stage alone causes the downstream preference.

Scope

American and British English provide a controlled comparison with well-documented contrasts. Other English varieties and the communities that use them require direct study rather than inference from this pair.
